This is part one of my four-part series about my experience doing an undergraduate degree in Artificial Intelligence and Computer Science at the University of Edinburgh from 2017 to 2022. I've been meaning to write this series for a while: partially to archive my experiences before I lose too much memory to the mists of time, and partially to help prospective students find out what life at uni is like. 

Academics were really important to me at uni: I chose Edinburgh in large part because it would let me take a broad range of courses (both CS and non-CS), and I wanted to do well so I could go on to a Masters or PhD later [^1]. 

## Background going into the degree

I started this degree with a fairly strong background:

- I did A-Level Maths, Further Maths, and AS Additional Further Maths at [Exeter Mathematics School](https://exetermathematicsschool.ac.uk/), which meant I was very comfortable with maths beyond the level expected for the program. 
- I had been fiddling with computers in one way or another since I was 11 or 12; I was very comfortable with Linux, knew how to program well in Python and had played with other languages (C, Java, Lisp, Go, Bash, BASIC...).
- I spent two summers before starting uni working in industry, first as a software developer in R&D (research and development), then as a data scientist.  

## Degree structure

The degree is four years long, with each year consisting of 120 credits split across two semesters. As a rough guide, 10 credits was between 80 and 100 hours of work across a 10-week semester. 

Edinburgh's degrees (much like other Scottish unis) are unique in that:
- It takes four, not three, years to finish the BSc.  
- You can study courses outside of your degree subject as part of your degree. This was important to me and I took as many non-CS courses as I could. 

### Year 1

This was the easiest year: the course contents were pretty gentle (especially given my background) and I had plenty of time to get used to uni life and do some part-time work.

Semester 1: 
- **Computation and Logic** (10 credits): DFAs and NFAs, regex, truth tables, deduction/resolution/satisfiability, formal logic. Definitely the hardest course of first year: it took until week 10, after a *lot* of hours of study, until I really understood it. 
- **Functional Programming** (10 credits): introduction to functional programming, taught in Haskell. Covered up to and including [Chapter 12 in Learn You a Haskell](https://learnyouahaskell.com/chapters). Fun course: I was already familiar with FP from learning Lisp, but learning Haskell led to a lot of interesting discoveries. I still sometimes use ideas from this course in my day-to-day work. 
- **Introduction to Linear Algebra** (20 credits): partially a repeat of A-Level maths (vectors, matrices, linear equations, eigenvalues/vectors), but with some new ideas introduced (vector spaces) and more focus on proof. Used Poole's Linear Algebra. I found this the trickier of the two maths courses but very manageable. 

Semester 2:
- **Object-Oriented Programming** (10 credits): introduction to Java and OOP concepts. This is the only course in my degree where I stopped attending part-way through, because it moved glacially slowly and did not teach me anything new. 
- **Data and Analysis** (10 credits): structured, semistructured, and unstructured data. Some XML, basic statistics, and SQL. The introduction to relational databases and SQL was really useful. This course has since been dropped from the curriculum, which is a shame. 
- **Calculus and its Applications** (20 credits): a repeat of A-Level maths, but again more of a focus on proofs. Used Stewart's Essential Calculus. Did not have any multi-variable calculus (and there are no other maths courses on the degree that would cover it), which I felt left me unprepared for the more maths-heavy ML courses in final year.

I also completed **Foundation Danish Language** (40 credits) as my outside course. This took me to CEFR level A2 and was very little work because it moved at such a slow pace. I wish I had taken Danish 1 instead, which moved faster, or German 1B, which I think would have been more challenging. However, what I learned in this class proved very helpful for learning Swedish to fluency a few years later.

### Year 2

The difficulty racheted up in year 2: partially because I started seeing new content I hadn't encountered before, and partially because we got a *lot* of coursework. There were no optional courses this year. 

Semester 1:
- **Discrete Mathematics and Mathematical Reasoning** (20 credits): sets, graphs, trees, modular arithmetic, big-O notation. Heavy emphasis on proof technique. Partially a repeat of the decision maths modules in A-Level, but with significantly more rigour. Probably my favourite course of the year and possibly of my degree. Used Rosen's Discrete Mathematics.  
- **Computer Systems** (10 credits): computer architecture, operating systems concepts. The coursework for this was brutal, mostly done in MIPS assembly with some C. All very useful concepts to learn. 
- **Introduction to Software Engineering** (10 credits): the software engineering lifecycle, UML, testing, and some detail on ethics, licensing, patents, etc. In this course I realized I enjoy academic software engineering much more than I like it in industry. 
- **Processing Formal and Natural Languages** (20 credits): formal languages (grammars and the Chomsky hierarchy), computability, parsers. A mixture of a broad course in theoretical CS and an introduction to NLP and compilers. Coming to uni, NLP was the area I was most interested in, but taking this course made me realize I liked the idea of it more than the practice. I really struggled getting my head around the theoretical CS side. 

Semester 2:
- **Probability with Applications** (20 credits): discrete and continuous probability, conditioning and independence, Markov chains, birth and death processes, queuing. Used Ross's A First Course in Probability. I really struggled with this course: probability is my weakest area of maths after mechanics, and it took a lot of long hours in the library to get a good (but not great) grade. 
- **Algorithms, Data Structures, Learning** (20 credits): a mixture of a DS&A course (big-O notation, sequential data structures, searching, sorting, graphs) and an introductory machine learning course (classification and regression, some basic NNs). I spent many long hours in the library getting my head around this course, but I really enjoyed it. This has now (correctly) been split into two separate courses. 
- **Reasoning and Agents** (20 credits): logic representation, agents, planning (HMMs, Bayesian networks). Used Russell and Norvig's AI: A Modern Approach. An interesting enough course in classical AI but I've never had any use for anything I covered here. This course no longer runs. 

In year 2 I also audited (attended lectures but did not submit coursework or sit the exam) a 10-credit introductory course in Statistics, which I was not allowed to take for credit alongside Probability with Applications. I originally took this intending to switch to a maths degree after year 2, because I was finding the maths courses more interesting and challenging than the CS courses. I struggled so much with Probability with Applications that in the end I stayed on the CS degree. 

### Year 3

Year 3 is where you start to tailor your modules to your interests. I intended to spend this year studying abroad but this fell through. This was the highest-workload year, mostly due to heavy coursework load. I took two optional courses this year: Foundations of Natural Language Processing and Database Systems. 


Semester 1:
- **Professional Issues** (10 credits): computing as a profession; social, ethical, legal, and commercial issues; project lifecycles and change management. I was one of the few people in the degree to enjoy this course. 
- **Informatics Large Practical** (20 credits): 100% coursework, building a system in Java: requirements analysis, programming, testing, and writeup. The assignment was to simulate a drone collecting items around a map. The workload for this course was unusually low - I finished the entire assignment within the first four weeks of term. 
- **Introductory Applied Machine Learning** (20 credits): a more in-depth introduction to machine learning (decision trees, SVMs, regression, NNs, naive Bayes, dimensionality reduction, (hierarchal) clustering, Gaussian Mixture Models, boosting, error functions, model evaluation). This course had way too little maths for a machine learning course, which meant further ML courses were a massive jump. It has (thankfully) since been updated to include much more theory. 
- **Database Systems** (20 credits): relational algebra and calculus, constraints and normal forms, transaction management (concurrency, conflict serializability, locking), SQL. I enjoyed this as an opportunity to practice SQL, though pacing was too slow for my taste. 


Semester 2:
- **Foundations of Natural Language Processing** (10 credits): a more in-depth look at the natural language topics from 2nd year (corpora; lexical, semantic, and syntactic processing). I took this purely as a prerequisite to take the more interesting NLP courses in year 4. It was interesting enough, but most of the algorithms are not used anymore.
- **Computer Security** (20 credits): network security, usable security/human factors, cryptography and cryprographic protocols, OS securty, malware, web security. Not an area I'm particularly interested in.
- **System Design Project** (20 credits): working in a group of 10 to build a robot. This course had a lot of potential but was poorly run and had a workload more like 40 credits. 


Courses I would have liked to take:
- Automated Reasoning: higher-order logic and theorem provers (Isabelle/HOL). I was blocked from taking this because its lectures were at the same time as Introductory Applied Machine Learning (which meant no-one on the AI degree could take it). 
- Algorithms and Data Structures: a broader and more in-depth version of the A&DS course in year 2. Would have been useful for interview prep, but I prioritised taking an NLP elective so I could do more NLP courses in final year.

### Year 4

I was supposed to switch to a MInf degree (five-year integrated Masters) at the start of this year, and spend all of fourth year abroad at Carnegie Mellon University. This was cancelled due to Covid. 

My initial plan for year 4 if I were to stay in Edinburgh was to load up on machine learning courses. For the first few weeks of term I was led to believe I would spend semester one at Edinburgh, semester two abroad, and year five back at Edinburgh, so I selected easy courses in semester one to leave me energy for hard courses at CMU in semester two, with the intention of taking all the hard ML courses in year five. By the time they officially cancelled the semester two exchange, it was too late to change courses to the ML choices, and I was too burnt out to stay on for year 5.

Whole year:
- **Honours Project** (40 credits): a year-long self-directed dissertation. This went poorly for me. Topic assignment was done randomly, and I got a project I wasn't well-suited for or interested in. This contributed a lot to me burning out in final year. I got an ok grade in the module but I had a very bad time.
- **Swedish 3 Language** (20 credits): a gentle-paced course going from B2 to C1 in Swedish. I took this for fun as extra credits. This course choice was not technically allowed in my degree program so I had to get College-level approval to do it.

Semester 1:
- **Engineering Project Management 4** (10 credits): topics in project management aimed at engineering students (stakeholder analysis, planning, critical path analysis / Gantt charts, contracts, project monitoring and control). I spent the summer before year 4 working as a Technical Project Manager in an engineering context so I took this outside course to get some theoretical background on the work I'd done. I enjoyed it, though very little is applicable to my day job.
- **Software Design and Modelling** (20 credits): more UML, model-driven development, design patterns, model transformations. Not much here was interesting, but an easy course to get a good grade in. I wish I'd taken something else. 
- **Formal Verification** (10 credits): CTL/LTL model checking, BDD, SAT and SMT solvers, temporal logic, SPARK and Ada. Probably one of my favourite courses of the year, and one of the few to grab my interest from a research perspective; I seriously considered applying for a PhD in FV after this. 


Semester 2:
- **Software Testing** (10 credits): broad survey of testing methods. Really easy course, probably not worth my time. 
- **Introduction to Theoretical Computer Science** (10 credits): computability: pushdown automata, Turing machines, register machines, undecidability, reductions; tractability: P, NP, hardnesss and completeness, Cook's theorem, P=NP, and the complexity hierarchy above NP; lambda calculus. I took this because I struggled with these topics in year 2. This module really belonged on a maths degree, because it assumed a lot more fluency with proof than Informatics students get through the degree. 
- **Natural Language Understanding, Generation, and Machine Translation** (20 credits): deep learning approaches to NLP, covering up to the state-of-the-art in a broad range of topics. I really enjoyed this course - it reawakened my teenage interest in NLP. 

Courses I would have liked to take:
- Computing in the Classroom: pedagogy of teaching computer science in primary and secondary school. 
- Machine Learning and Pattern Recognition: a very maths-heavy look at machine learning fundamentals. 
- Probabilistic Modelling and Reasoning: probabilistic models, inference. 
- Machine Learning Theory: rigorous treatment of topics in machine learning (robustness, correctness, accuracy, complexity, privacy...).
- Machine Learning Practical: building machine learning projects in PyTorch/TensorFlow.
- Advanced Database Systems: deep-dive into the internal function of database systems.  
- Logic, Computability and Incompleteness: first-order logic and theory of computability. I enrolled on ITCS instead, because I had already hit the limit of courses outside the Informatics degree. 
- Text Technologies for Data Science: information retrieval and text classification. 


## About the academic experience

So what is doing a degree actually like?

I usually worked at least 60 hours a week: of this, 20 was contact hours, and 40 was self-study. At exam time or during overlapping periods of coursework this was higher. This is the upper end of workload among my cohort; I think it would be possible to do closer to 20-25 hours of self-study a week and finish with a 2:1 or a first, but I was aiming for the highest grades I could get. Balancing this amount of studying alongside part-time work, extracurricular activities, and looking for internships was really, really hard. 

Contact hours covers:
- Lectures. 
- Tutorials: you complete tutorial exercises beforehand and spend an hour going through them with course staff. I really enjoyed tutorials and I was one of the few people who attended them regularly.  
- Labs: drop-in sessions for working on coursework and getting help from course staff. I attended very few of these because I didn't need the help. 

Self-study is the bulk of the degree. This means:
- Hours in the library understanding concepts from lecture slides, textbooks, and the internet. 
- Completing coursework. I felt quite a lot of coursework at Edinburgh was assigned for the sake of having work to do, rather than to help with learning; most of it was busywork. If I were to redo uni I would put a lot less effort into coursework and only do the bits I felt were interesting / helping me learn.
