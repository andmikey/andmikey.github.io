This is part two of my four-part series about my experience doing an undergraduate degree in Artificial Intelligence and Computer Science at the University of Edinburgh from 2017 to 2022. 

At uni, I had three goals centred around work experience:
1. Become as employable as possible: I figured the best way to do this was getting as much work experience as possible.
2. Figure out what I liked and didn't like doing, to best inform my choice of career coming out of uni. 
3. Earn some money. 

## Prologue: working in civil service

In year 12 (age 16), I had the opportunity to interview for a summer internship as an R&D Software Engineer at the [UK Hydrographic Office](https://www.admiralty.co.uk/). I did well in the interview and spent the summer between year 12 and year 13 working there. I built a small proof-of-concept project using NLP to automatically convert Notices to Mariners from text format into points and bounding boxes that could be imported to GIS software (e.g. QGIS). It was my first taste of professional life and I *loved* it. 

In year 13 I applied again, this time for a summer internship as an R&D Data Scientist. Again, I did well in the interview, and spent the summer between year 13 and the first year of university in the newly-formed data science team. I helped productionize [a system to detect and classify offshore objects from satellite imagery](https://ukhodigital.blog.gov.uk/2018/08/15/taking-part-in-sprint-18/), getting a taste of deep learning and the challenges of production-quality data science. I enjoyed my time here even more, and had an offer to stay on with the team, but I decided to head off to uni and explore other careers first. 

I was very lucky to get these experiences before I started university, and they meant I had an unusually strong CV for a first-year.

## Year 1: sysadmin @ UoE

I found the workload of first year manageable enough that I decided to get a part-time job in semester two of first year. I joined the university's Unix team, working 15 hours a week to help manage the Linux/Unix estate. This was partially second-line helpdesk work, and partially working on a project to replace the university's existing CA issuance process with [Let's Encrypt](https://letsencrypt.org/). It was nice to work professionally with Linux after spending my teenage years toying with it, but I didn't see a future for myself in sysadmin work. I left this job at the end of semester two to start my internship at Heineken.

## Year 1: data science @ Heineken

I had a very tough time finding internships in first year, because most companies were only looking for penultimate year students. I started applying/interviewing in September of first year and it took until late May to get an offer, working at Heineken as a Data Scientist. My summer project was building a system that used NLP and ML to automatically match product names between two different databases (think: knowing that "Heineken 6x330ml" and "Heineken six-pack, 330ml ea" are referring to the same thing). I had a really fun time! With my previous roles being in civil service, it was a weird mental switch to working in a department and role where business impact was important. I learned a lot. 

## Year 2: BI/ops @ Amazon

After my first taste of commercial work, I wanted to try something that was even more business-focused. I ended up joining Amazon in a role that was a mixture of business intelligence (mostly PowerBI and Excel) and project management/operations, working in inbound supply chain. I didn't enjoy the work too much, but I felt I vibed well with Amazon's culture. I was supposed to have a return offer to join a different team the next summer but both my manager and my assigned HR contact left the company, so I fell through the cracks.  

## Year 2/3: tutoring @ UoE

To cover my living costs in year 2 and 3 I worked part-time as an academic tutor for my department, running tutorials for first- and second-year courses. I realized I really love teaching people! I wish I'd done more tutoring but it was quite intellectually demanding so I could only do it during semesters where the course workload was lower. 

## Year 3: data science @ Arm

Covid cancelled a lot of internship programs this year, which made it hard to find something. I eventually accepted an offer at Arm, working as a data scientist in their R&D department. My project was implementing an algorithm to reduce the amount of data needed to train GPU verification models. I read a lot of papers and implemented some algorithms straight from research papers in Python... then spent ages trying to get them to run at a reasonable speed! I spent the entire summer working remotely and realized that I prefer working in the office. I had an offer to stay on for another 12 months during my gap year, but decided instead to move to Germany for six months and work at SAP. 

## Gap year: ML research @ SAP

Loads of people - including me - assumed I would go on to do a PhD after finishing my undergrad. I'd been applying for research roles on-and-off since first year but never got anywhere because almost all of them were aimed at people who already had research experience. After a referral and a bit of luck, I managed to get an offer to spend six months at SAP's security research lab in Germany during my gap year. I worked on adding differential privacy guarantees to variational autoencoders for text anonymization, resulting in a [joint-first-author paper](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3485447.3512232). My supervisor was great and the project was interesting, but I realized that research life is not for me:

- I struggle to get motivated by working on problems that have a long or unclear route to impact. 
- I am not as good at research as I am at commercial work. Investing 4+ years into a PhD to be an average or below-average researcher doesn't make sense when I can go into industry and progress much faster.
- I found the structure of research labs difficult. Having spent so long working in industry, moving from working in a team to working on a solo project with mentorship from just one person was tricky and felt lonely. I think this was partially to do with the way the lab was structured and Covid meaning the whole lab was remote, but I also think it's a structural thing around how research labs in general are set up. 
- ML/NLP research seemed to be in a bad state. Loads of papers seemed to be minor changes on existing work trying to eke out another few percentage points over state-of-the-art. It was very hard to pull out interesting work from the noise, and the interesting work was obscured by pages and pages of needlessly verbose or obtuse notation.

## Gap year: TPM/ops @ Northvolt

After working at SAP, I was originally intending to take a break of a few months before term started, but this internship at Northvolt (a startup building green batteries to decarbonize Europe) came up and I couldn't resist. I moved to Sweden and spent four months working as a technical project manager, helping to improve data processes for battery validation. I really enjoyed working at Northvolt - the people were highly motivated and the projects were interesting, and I got to travel around Sweden a bit. I intended to come back here for my grad job, but my offer fell through and Northvolt has not done well since. I did apply to a few grad jobs in PM but eventually realized the TPM role here was closer to startup ops than it traditional PM work. I ended up going into data science but I'd pretty happily go back to ops. 

## Some stumbles on the way 

I applied for a *lot* of jobs and internships during my studies. I would estimate I spent 5-10 hours a week during term time purely on job-hunting (finding vacancies, writing a cover letter, tuning my CV, doing phone screens / takehomes / on-sites). I just about managed to secure internships every summer, but there were a lot of stumbles along the way:

- I applied for plenty of big names (Google, Twitter, Facebook, Microsoft, etc) every year and I never got past CV screening. 
- I flunked more interviews than I can count. Sometimes this was because I hadn't done the courses that they assumed I had knowledge of (particularly algos / data structures). Sometimes I flunked them on purpose because I quickly got the vibe the team would be a bad fit. 
- I dropped out of quite a few processes because I didn't believe I was good enough to pass the final stage.
- I hate doing Leetcode and I hate preparing for Leetcode interviews. These days I just avoid companies that I know ask for it. 
- I turned down a handful of offers. Often this was offers from small companies / startups where I felt I wouldn't learn much (shoutout to the company which was almost entirely staffed by interns on 3/6 month placements!), but sometimes companies where I went through the interview process but got bad vibes about the culture / the work I would be doing. 

## And after graduation...

I considered a lot of different options for grad jobs:

- Tech-y jobs: software engineering, data engineering, data science, data analysis, business intelligence. 
- Business-y jobs: product management, project management, consulting, generic "ops" roles in startups. 
- Left-field options: patent law, actuarial science, teaching. 

After a lot of thinking about it, I ended up going back to where I started and deciding to work in data science. Finding a grad job was tricky. The market was in a bad place post-Covid, and 'only' having a Bachelors degree put me at a disadvantage for data science jobs. Having lots of relevant commercial experience on my CV helped some with getting interviews, but I still got filtered out from a lot of roles where I think I would have done well, simply because I didn't have a postgraduate degree. 

I now work at a financial services company, building ML models to detect financial fraud. It's really interesting work and uses a lot of the skills I developed during university; it also aligns nicely with my view of what a good career should be.
